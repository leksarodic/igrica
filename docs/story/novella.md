# The Long Note

*A novel of Carrow Vale*

---

## 1. The Road Down

The road into Carrow Vale had twelve switchbacks, and Wren Alk counted them the way her father had taught her to count anything that mattered: out loud, under her breath, so the counting had a shape.

By the ninth, the valley came up to meet her. Wet stone, cold reed, woodsmoke. Water moving under a skin of ice. Then the whole long bowl of the place, terraced and brown and gold, with the town stacked in its center like cups on a shelf. The river ran through it in a silver braid. On the highest step, the petrified wood caught the last light and threw it back in thin, wrong colors.

Wren set down her pack. The copper canister was warm against her ribs, which was a lie; it had been four days since the cremation. She kept it wrapped in her father's coat anyway, because the coat still smelled of tar and pipe smoke and the particular sourness of a man who lived his life on boats and never learned to swim.

She had heard the note all her life, a low pressure behind the eyes that became a sound only when she stopped moving. Most people called it the wind in the rocks. Her mother had called it the undertone. Wren had spent six years in the Low Water convincing herself it was nothing, and the valley had waited patiently to prove her wrong.

It was thinner than she remembered. Not silent. Thin, like a wire strung too tight and asked to carry too much.

At the gatehouse a Tender told her the Tending was in three days and the Warden had shut the west road because the Hush was moving. She looked at the canister and did not ask whose it was.

"Don't answer it," she said.

"I know the rule."

"Everyone knows the rule. People still answer." She held the gate. "Welcome home, Listener."

"I'm not a Listener."

The woman said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. West of the river mouth, where the lowland should have been green, the grey had come closer than her father's maps.

The town came into view at the eleventh switchback: the dry basin where the waterfall had once fallen, the terraces of ochre houses, the copper roofs gone green at the seams. Above it all stood the Hollow Bell, the great stone tower shaped like a bell by someone who had never seen one, cracked from lip to shoulder and still standing out of habit. The Long Bell hung inside. Wren could not see it, but she could feel it, a second note under the first, deeper, older, the sound of a thing being held up by many hands.

She had been eight when she last heard it ring properly. She remembered the sound filling the valley like water fills a bowl, her mother lifting her off her feet in the square, Tam beside them with his mouth open in delight. Nothing could be that loud and not be alive.

Now the note was thin, and the town was thin too: shuttered windows, weeds in the terraces, smoke rising straight and sparse. At the twelfth switchback a child ran past her chasing a kettle-bird. The bird was round and brown and humming a fifth. The child was laughing. Then the bird veered, and the child stopped, and both of them looked west, where the grey was.

She found the Warden in the tower yard.

Sable Voss was fifty-eight: tall, straight-backed, grey hair pulled so tight it seemed to pull her face with it. She wore the slate coat of the Tenders, with copper bells down the spine, and a copper plate over her left ear, worked with holes like a sieve.

"Vess, if that rope slips again I will feed you to the reeds," she said.

"Warden."

Sable turned. Her face did not change, which was worse than if it had. "Wren Alk."

"I brought my father home."

"I heard." Sable looked at the canister. "Corin was a good man. Loud."

"He wanted the reed rite."

"He wanted a lot of things." Sable's voice was dry and precise, sanded down at every edge. "The reed rite is for Reedfolk. We can give him the bell rite. He earned it. He kept the ferry running through the bad year."

"He didn't believe in the bell."

"Corin didn't believe in anything he couldn't argue with." Sable turned back to the scaffolding. "The offer stands. The Tending is in three days. It would be a good death."

Wren wanted to hit something. Instead she said, "He asked to be put in the reeds at the north channel. He made me write it down."

"Then put him in the reeds," Sable said, without turning. "I'm not the warden of your father."

That was the thing about Sable Voss. She never gave you the argument you were ready for.

The bell rang the ninth hour at midnight. On the seventh stroke it faltered.

Wren was awake. She heard the note thin, not stop, thin, like a hand losing its grip on a rope. The last strokes came back stronger, but the damage was done. In the street, doors opened. Someone called out.

By the time Wren reached the tower yard, a small crowd had gathered. Sable stood on the steps with a lantern.

"The damp," she said. "It will be adjusted."

A man in a baker's apron said it had never done that. Sable told him it had, and that he had not been listening. Wren did not laugh. She stood at the back and listened to the note underneath the bell, the one nobody else seemed to hear, and it was not the damp. It was a voice, somewhere down inside the tower, pressing up against the stone like water against a hull.

She walked back to the Listening House and did not sleep.
## 2. Reedwater

The north channel ran through the reed beds like a seam through cloth. In her father's time it had been wide enough for the ferry at any tide; now it was a green-brown trickle between drowned banks, and the reeds on either side were grey at the tips.

Nissa Quill was sitting on the ferry with her boots in the water, mending a rope.

"Ferry's closed," she called, without looking up. "Channel's choked. Unless you want to walk on water, which I don't recommend. The crawlers are bad this year."

"I'll walk. On the bank."

Nissa looked up. She was nineteen, brown as a reed stem, with a quick, crooked face and river-glass beads in her hair.

"Alk," she said.

"Quill."

"You look like your mother." She said it without pity, which Wren appreciated. "You're going to the north channel to put him in the reeds."

"Yes."

"Then you'll need a boat, and my boat needs a channel, and the channel needs someone to cut the choke." Nissa stood and stretched, her back cracking. "You can help. The reeds don't care about your grief, but they'll take your hands."

They worked until noon, cutting dead reed and grey silt and something Nissa called hush-mold, which grew where the sound had gone wrong. At the far end of the choke, Nissa's pole struck stone.

It was a flat slab of pale limestone, smooth as a table. Wren scraped away the silt. Underneath were marks—three lines, a circle, and a row of small cuts along one edge.

"Cantor," Nissa said. "There's a whole wall of them down by the old sluice."

Wren traced the marks. Three notes, she thought, though she could not have said why.

The stone hummed. It was faint, a mosquito in the next room, but Wren felt it in her teeth. She closed her eyes. The hum resolved into a chord: three notes, close together, the third one flattened. It was a chord that wanted to be resolved and had been waiting a long time.

"You hear something," Nissa said.

"Three notes."

"Sing them."

"I can't sing."

"Everyone can sing. It's the Warden who can't." Nissa grinned. "Go on. Quietly. The reeds don't judge."

Wren sang. Her voice was rough from disuse, but the notes came out true, and the stone answered. A thin line of gold light ran through the carved marks, then faded.

Nissa took a cord from her belt, tied three knots in it at different spacings, and hung it around her neck.

"Map," she said. "My mother had one. She used to say it was the only map that never lied."

They finished clearing the channel at dusk. The water began to move again, carrying the grey silt downstream. Nissa poled the ferry out, and Wren stood at the bow with the canister, and when they reached the north channel, where the reeds turned silver and the water ran clear, she opened it.

Corin Alk went into the water in a grey cloud that the current took gently, spreading it through the roots. Wren said the things he had asked her to say.

"He liked the sound of the ferry rope against the pole. He liked rain on a copper roof. He liked the way the kettle-birds argued in the morning, before anyone else was awake." Her voice caught. "He liked my mother's laugh. He said it was the only sound in the valley that couldn't be tuned."

Nissa stood beside her with her hat in her hands. The reeds moved. Somewhere out in the grey water, a bird called once and stopped.

"Go on," Nissa said quietly. "That's not all of it."

"He lied to me about Tam," Wren said. "He told me Tam went to the Low Water. For years. And I believed him because I wanted to."

The water took that too.

They were tying up at the dock when the voice came out of the reeds.

"Wren."

It was a child's voice. It was Tam's voice, the way he had sounded at fourteen, cracked and eager, calling her in from the river because supper was ready. Wren's whole body turned toward it before her mind caught up. Nissa grabbed her wrist hard enough to hurt.

"Don't," Nissa said. "Don't answer."

"It's him."

"It isn't." Nissa's face was white. "Look at the reeds."

The reeds were still. Not wind-still; wrong-still, as if the air had been taken out of them. Beyond them, twenty paces into the channel, the grey was moving against the current, a slow curl of absence like smoke in water.

"Wren," the voice said again, and this time the vowel was too long. It was a copy made by someone who had heard the name once.

Nissa pulled her back. The ferry pole came up out of the water, and something had hold of the end of it. Nissa drew her knife and cut the rope. The pole spun away into the grey and vanished without a splash.

They ran. The voice followed them to the edge of the reeds, calling Wren's name in her brother's mouth, and then it stopped, as if it had run out of breath.

At the dock, Nissa sat on the boards and shook.

"That's the third time this month," she said. "It's never been this close to town."

"What is it?"

"The Hush. Or something in it. My mother said it isn't a place. She said it's a debt. When a sound is taken and not given back, the debt sits there, and it gets cold, and it starts to want."

"I heard the chord," Wren said. "On the stone. Three notes."

Nissa stood, brushed the mud off her knees, and picked up her hat.

"Come to the ferry house," she said. "I'll make tea. And you can tell me what else your mother wrote, and I'll decide whether to be frightened."
## 3. The Bell-Founder

Brannoc Kettle's foundry stood at the bottom of the quarry road, where the noise could not bother anyone who mattered: a low building of black stone with a copper roof gone green. Wren heard the bell before she saw the door, ringing in a cast-iron frame with the patience of a man testing a thing he did not trust.

Inside, the heat hit her like a wall. Brannoc was at the furnace with his back to her, a huge old man in a leather apron, his bald head shining, a copper horn curving from his ear to his mouth. A kettle-bird sat on a beam above him, round and brown, humming a fifth.

"Shut the door," Brannoc said, without turning. "You're letting the cold in."

"Wren Alk." He set the crucible down and turned. His face was broad and weathered, with a nose that had been broken and a mouth that had decided to be amused. "You look like your mother. Everyone's going to keep saying that. Get used to it. This is Pip. She steals. Don't leave anything shiny."

"I need to know what the Note is made of," Wren said.

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment. Then he picked up a small bell from the bench, no bigger than an apple, and held it out.

"Hold this."

It was warm and heavy and slightly rough, the surface pitted with tiny holes.

"Humstone," Brannoc said. "From the deep quarry. The Cantors called it the singing stone. You cast it into a bell and the bell remembers." He tapped the rim with his fingernail, and the note that came out was clear and low. "Every bell remembers something. A good founder can make it remember a place, a season, a person. The Cantors could make it remember a voice."

Wren turned the bell over in her hands. Under the fading note, faint, was something else.

"Say something," Brannoc said. "It helps the stone settle."

"I don't know what to say."

"Then listen."

She listened. And the bell, which had no business doing anything but ringing, spoke.

*Wren. Wren, come in, supper's ready.*

She dropped it. The bell hit the stone floor and bounced, and Brannoc caught it with a foot before it rolled into the furnace. Pip shrieked and flew to the rafters.

"That was Tam."

Brannoc straightened, the bell cradled in his huge hands. His face had gone carefully blank, which told her more than any answer.

"That was my brother's voice. He's been dead nine years. That bell is new."

"It isn't new. It's a practice bell. I've been casting from the same humstone for twenty years."

"Then why is he in it?"

Brannoc set the bell down on the bench, very gently, as if it were a sleeping animal. He took the copper horn from his ear, polished it with his thumb, then put it back.

"Because the stone remembers," he said. "And because nine years ago, when the Hush came up the valley and took four houses in one night, the Warden came to me and asked for a jar. Humstone, sealed with wax, tuned to a voice. She had one of your brother's. He'd been calling for you, they said. Out in the fog. They caught the last of it before the Hush took him." Brannoc looked at her. "She fed it to the Long Bell. It held the Note for another year. Maybe two."

Wren could feel the sweat running down her back, and under it a cold that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"You knew."

"I knew."

"You knew, and you let my father believe Tam went to the Low Water."

"Your father knew too." Brannoc's voice was not unkind, which made it worse. "Not the jar. He knew Tam was taken. He couldn't say it, so he said the other thing. That's how it works here. We all know, and we say the other thing, and the Note keeps ringing."

"And my mother?"

Brannoc was silent.

"Did she know?"

"She knew better than anyone. Mira came to me the year before she left. She wanted a set of chimes. Twelve, tuned to a chord. She said the Note was a single voice holding up a whole valley, and a single voice was a kind of tyranny, and she was going to teach the valley to sing instead."

"Did you make them?"

"I made her three." He nodded at a shelf high on the wall, where three small bells sat in a row, dusty and silent. "Then the Warden found out and told me to stop. I stopped. Mira went into the Hush a month later. I've been looking at those bells for seven years. Wondering."

Wren walked to the shelf and took one down. It was lighter than the practice bell, tuned higher, with a small mark on the rim: three cuts, like the marks on the channel stone.

"She left something for you," Brannoc said. "She told me to keep it until someone came who could use it."

He went to the back of the foundry and returned with a tuning fork, plain copper, worn smooth at the handle. It was warm from his hand.

"It doesn't do anything. Not anymore. But she said you'd know what it was for."

Wren struck the fork against the bench. It rang, a clean high note, and the three little bells answered, all at once, a chord that was not quite the one on the stone but close, a cousin to it.

"Ask Sable," Brannoc said. "Ask her what the Tending is really for. Then ask yourself why you came back."

"I came back to bury my father."

"No," Brannoc said. "You came back to hear the bell."
## 4. What Sable Kept

The Listening House had been a cistern once, cut into the rock beneath the third terrace: a round room with a domed ceiling and a floor of black water. The water had been drained four hundred years ago and replaced with shelves. There were wax tablets, paper books, copper plates, and one shelf of jars that Pell did not let anyone touch.

Pell was waiting with tea and a face that had decided to be brisk.

"Sit. Eat. You look like a scarecrow with a grudge."

Wren told him about the stone, the chord, the voice in the reeds, the jar Brannoc had made, the tuning fork in her pocket.

Pell listened with his eyes closed. When she finished, he opened them and said, "You've had a busy week."

"I want the truth."

"The truth is a large building with many rooms," Pell said. "Most people want a particular room and call it the truth." He went to the shelf of jars and took down a flat wooden box. Inside were six wax tablets, cracked and dark with age.

"These are copies. The originals are in the Undersong, where I am too old to go. The Cantors came to the valley six hundred years ago: surveyors, engineers, people who liked to measure things. They found the Undersong, the great cavity under the valley, and they found that it resonated. A note sung into the right stone would travel for miles. A note sung into the wrong stone would bring the roof down. So they built the Chimeworks. Channels cut to length, stone chimes on the ridges, the great bell at the center. They tuned the valley like an instrument."

"What happened?"

"The First Dissonance. An earthquake, or a mistake, or both. A crack opened in the Chimeworks, and the Hush came through it. The Cantors tried to retune. It didn't hold. Then one of them, a conductor named Ilse Vey, discovered something." Pell's voice was flat. "A voice given to the bell strengthens the Note. Not a song. A voice. A person's whole voice, taken at the moment of dying, or grief, or terror, when it is most itself."

Wren set down her cup. "Taken."

"Harvested was the word they used. The first were volunteers. Criminals, debtors, the old and the willing. It was called the Gift. Then the Hush came again, and the volunteers were not enough, and the Gift became a levy, and the levy became a hunt. They took people, Wren. They silenced them and put their voices in the bell, and the valley was safe, and everyone agreed not to say the word for it. For fifteen years. Then the Long Silence."

"The Long Silence."

"The Cantors stopped speaking. All of them, at once, as if a hand had closed over the valley. The records say they went into the Undersong and sealed the door. Some say they were ashamed. Some say they went mad. The town was founded by the ones who stayed above, and they had a choice: tell the truth and lose the Note, or keep the Note and forget. They chose to forget. They called it the Tending."

Wren looked at the shelf of jars. "And the voices are still in there."

"Some. The Note has been fed, on and off, for four hundred years. Not always by force. Sometimes by grief. Sometimes by a family who thought it was an honor. The Tenders keep the jars. The Warden decides who is asked. Most years, no one is. The Note holds. The spring feeds the bell, the bell feeds the valley, and everyone sleeps."

"The spring is failing," Wren said. "And Sable has called a full Tending."

"Yes."

Wren laid her mother's notebook on the table. "My mother wrote that the Note was a debt. She wrote that the Hush is what the debt leaves behind. She wrote—" She found the page. "'A chord, not a note. Many voices, willing. The bell must be given back, not fed.'"

Pell read it slowly, his lips moving. When he looked up, his eyes were wet.

"I told her not to go. I told her the Hush does not give people back. She said she wasn't going to ask it to. She found the three notes. She found the door in the Undersong. She went in with a lantern and a rope and a jar of her own voice, and she came out four days later, and she was not the same. She said the Hush was full of voices. She said they were not angry. She said they were lonely. She said the Choirmaster was keeping them, and it did not know how to let go, and neither did she."

"The Choirmaster."

"Ilse Vey. Or what is left of her. The Cantors' conductor, still conducting. Mira went back a month later. She said she had one more thing to give. She left you with your aunt, walked west, and the Hush took her, and it did not give her back."

Wren sat very still. Above them, the Long Bell rang the hour, and the note was thin.

"Why did she leave me?"

"Because she thought she was coming back. Because she was wrong. People are wrong, Wren. It is the most human thing about us."

Pell stood, went to a drawer, and came back with a heavy iron key.

"The old sluice gate. It opens onto the upper gallery. The Tenders have the other key, and they will not give it to you. Be back before the Tending. And Wren—do not answer anything down there that knows your name."

The tower stairs were narrow and cold, and they turned around the bell without touching it. The Long Bell was above her in the dark, a huge bronze throat, and the note it made was not a sound so much as a pressure against her chest.

Sable waited at the top, on the platform that circled the bell's shoulder. She had lit four lanterns. In their light the bell was green-black, mottled with age, its lip worked with a pattern of reeds and waves.

"You should not be here," Sable said.

"Brannoc told me about the jar."

Sable's face did not change. She looked out through the tower's open shoulder at the valley, at the grey line to the west.

"Brannoc talks too much."

"He told me you fed my brother to the bell."

"I did."

The plainness of it stopped Wren's breath. She had expected denial, or justification, or the slow careful language Sable used with the town. She had not expected *I did*, offered like a bill.

"Why?"

"Because the Hush took four houses that year. Because the Note was failing, because the spring was low, because I had a jar with a boy's voice in it, and because I had a choice between a boy who was already gone and a town that was not." Sable turned to face her. "I have made that choice eleven times in thirty years. I remember every name. I do not expect you to forgive me. I expect you to understand that I would do it again."

"He was fourteen."

"He was fourteen, and he was calling for you, and I decided his calling would do more good in the bell than in the air." Sable's voice was steady. "You want me to be a monster. It would be easier for you. I am not. I am a woman holding a door shut, and you are angry that I used the wood that was available."

Wren's hands were shaking. She put them behind her back.

"Did my mother know?"

"She knew about the jar. She did not know I had used it until later. She came to me the year before she left. She asked me to open the bell. She said the voices were not a foundation, they were a debt, and the valley was living on borrowed air. She had a plan. A chord. Many voices, willing." The Warden's voice turned flat. "I told her no. The Note was holding. The Hush was quiet. I told her she would kill us all with a pretty idea."

"And then she went into the Hush."

"And then she went into the Hush." Sable looked at the bell. "I have wondered every day since whether I sent her there."

"You did."

"Yes." Sable nodded slowly. "I did."

"The Tending is in two days," she said. "The spring is the lowest it has been in living memory. The Hush has moved three hundred paces up the west road since midsummer. I have one jar left that might hold, and it is not enough. The Note needs a voice that can carry. It needs a Listener."

Wren laughed. It was not a good laugh.

"You want me to die in a bell."

"I want you to consider it. That is all. The town will decide. The Tending is not mine to command, whatever you think." Sable's voice softened, just slightly. "Your mother would have understood the arithmetic."

"My mother would have broken the arithmetic," Wren said. "That was the whole point of her."

Sable was silent for a long moment. Then she said, "Yes. I know."

Wren turned around. "You are asking me to be a lie."

"I am asking you to be a foundation," Sable said. "There is a difference, and I am no longer sure it is a good one."

Wren left her on the platform with the lanterns and the bell. On the stairs, halfway down, she had to stop and put her hand against the wall, because the note was in her teeth and her chest, and her brother's voice was somewhere inside it, saying her name in a copy that was almost right.

She did not answer. She went down into the town.
## 5. The Undersong

The old sluice gate was under the north terrace, behind a curtain of ivy trained, once, into the shape of a bell. Pell's key turned with a groan. The gate opened onto a stair that went down into cold air and the sound of water.

Nissa came with her. Wren had not asked. She had appeared at the dock with two lanterns, a coil of rope, and a knife, and said, "If you're going to do something stupid, I'm going to be there to tell you it was stupid."

The stair descended for a long time. The walls changed from brick to cut stone to raw limestone. The sound of the town faded, then the river, then everything except their footsteps and the water.

At the bottom, the sluice opened into a gallery.

Wren had expected a cave. What she found was a cathedral: a ceiling thirty feet high, worked with ribs of pale stone, a floor of black water still as glass, and a narrow walkway along one wall. Columns rose from the water at intervals, each hollow, each humming a different note. The notes together made a chord so low and wide that Wren felt it in her bones before she heard it.

"The Cantors built this," Nissa whispered.

"They tuned it. Every column is a note. The whole room is a chord."

"Then why does it feel like a held breath?"

Because it was, Wren thought. The chord was not resolving. It had been holding the same three notes for four hundred years, and the stone was tired.

They walked along the narrow way. At the far end stood an arch of pale stone filled with a grey that was not fog and not water, a grey that moved slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Beyond the arch was a round room, and the room was full of jars.

They stood on shelves cut into the walls, hundreds of them, thousands, each a small sealed vessel of humstone with a copper lid. Each one hummed. The sound was not a chorus; it was a crowd, each voice on its own note, together making a noise like a city heard from a hill.

"Nissa," Wren said. "These are people."

Nissa had stopped at the entrance. Her face was grey. "There's something in here with us."

The Choirmaster came out of the dark at the far end of the room.

It was tall, taller than the arch, and thin as a tuning fork. It wore a robe of grey that moved without wind. Its face was a smooth bell-curve plate with no eyes, no mouth, only a faint seam where a mouth might have been. Its hands ended in long prongs of pale stone, and when it moved, it made no sound at all.

"Visitors," it said.

The voice was not one voice. It was a chord of voices, some old, some young, some speaking a half-beat behind the others, so that the word arrived in pieces and assembled itself in Wren's ears.

"We don't get visitors. The water is cold. Come in. Count the steps. Don't answer."

Wren's blood went cold. Those were her father's words, her mother's words, her own words, borrowed and stacked.

"What are you?"

"I am the one who keeps them. I am the one who listens. I am the one who does not forget. Would you like to hear your brother? He is here. He has been waiting such a long time."

"Don't," Nissa said.

The Choirmaster raised one pronged hand. On a shelf near Wren, a jar began to glow.

"Wren," said the jar, in Tam's voice. "Wren, come in, supper's ready."

Wren's knees went weak. She put her hand on the shelf to steady herself, and the jar was warm under her fingers, warm like a living thing.

"He is not in there," the Choirmaster said, almost gently. "Only the shape of him. The part that called. The rest has gone on. But the shape is enough, isn't it? It is always enough. That is what we are for."

"You're a prison," Wren said.

"We are a memory. The valley could not bear to lose them. We could not bear to lose them. So we kept them. Is that so wrong?"

"Yes," Nissa said. "If they didn't choose it."

"Choice." The word came out in a dozen voices, some bitter, some sad, some amused. "The living are so fond of that word. The dead are not consulted."

Wren looked at the jar. It was still glowing faintly, and she could hear her brother's voice fading back into the hum.

"I'm sorry," she said, to the jar, to the room, to the thousands of jars. "I'm sorry no one asked you."

The Choirmaster moved. It was faster than anything that tall should move, and suddenly it was between them and the door, prongs spread.

"Stay," it said. "Stay and sing. The choir is incomplete. It has been incomplete for so long."

Nissa threw her lantern.

It was not a clever plan. It was the plan of a terrified woman with a lantern and good aim. The glass broke against the Choirmaster's chest, oil caught, and the grey robe went up in a sheet of flame. The Choirmaster did not scream. It stood there burning, and its voices said, in a chorus of borrowed mouths, "Oh. Oh, that is warm."

Wren grabbed a jar from the shelf, the nearest one, and ran.

They fled through the cathedral of columns with the burning Choirmaster behind them, and the chord rose around them like water, and the jars rang in sympathy, a wave of sound that followed them up the stair and into the cold air of the sluice. The gate slammed shut. Nissa threw the bolt.

The jar in Wren's arms was warm. It was humming.

"What did you take?" Nissa whispered.

Wren looked at the copper lid. There was a name scratched into it, old and faint.

"Not Tam," she said. "Someone else."
## 6. Glasswood

Glasswood had been a pine forest once. Now the trees were stone: silver-grey trunks without bark, branches like frozen lightning, a canopy of shards that split the light. Nothing grew on the ground except glassgrass, which rang faintly when the wind moved it. Sound behaved strangely there; Wren's footsteps came back a half-beat late, in a slightly different voice.

They found Mira's camp at midday, under a stone pine: a bedroll, a cold fire, a pot, a lantern with the glass cracked, and a tin box with a latch. The box held a notebook, a compass, a coil of knotted cord, and a letter.

The letter was addressed to Wren. She sat down in the dry grass and read it while Nissa stood watch. The handwriting was her mother's: small, exact, the letters leaning forward as if they were impatient.

*My Wren,*

*If you are reading this, I did not come back, or I came back wrong, or I came back and could not say what I had learned. I am sorry for all three.*

*The Note is a debt. The Hush is what the debt leaves behind. When a voice is taken and not returned, the silence it leaves does not disappear; it gathers. It gets cold. It wants. The Cantors thought they were holding the Hush back with the Note, but they were feeding it. Every voice in the bell is a hole in the world, and the Hush is the shape of the holes.*

*The Hush is not a place you can close. It is a place you can pay. You pay it by giving back what was taken. Not all at once. A voice at a time, if you must. You sing the name of the person who was taken, and you sing the chord, and you let them go. The Choirmaster will fight you. It is not evil, Wren. It is a conductor who has been holding the same chord for four hundred years and has forgotten how to end it. It is lonely. Do not hate it. Do not stay with it.*

*The chord is three notes. You will find them on the Cantor stones. You have already heard them, I think. The third is flattened. It is not a beautiful chord. It is a chord that wants to resolve. That is the point.*

*I came here to give back the voices one at a time. I have given back eleven. There are thousands. I cannot do it alone, and I cannot leave, because every voice I give back makes the Hush thinner and makes me thinner too. I am still here. I am not sure I am still your mother. I am sure I love you. Those two things can both be true.*

*Do not give your voice to the bell. Not for the valley, not for Sable, not for me. If the town will not sing, then let the Note fail and let the valley change. A valley that lives by taking children is not worth the air it borrows. You are worth more than a foundation. You are a person, and you are my daughter, and I am sorry I left you to find that out alone.*

*Sable is not a monster. She is a woman who chose the arithmetic because no one would help her choose anything else. Tell her I said so. Tell her I forgave her before I left. Tell her the door is open now, and the voices are still coming home, and some of them are angry, and that is all right.*

*If you can, sing at the Tending. Not into the bell. Sing with the town. The bell will do what it does. The valley will do what it does. You will do what you do.*

*I love you. I am proud of you. Put me in the reeds, not the bell.*

*— Mira*

Wren read it three times. The third time, she cried, which she had not done at the funeral, or at the gate, or in the tower. Nissa did not say anything. She stood with her back to Wren and watched the silver trees, and after a while she came and sat down and put her arm around her.

"We can still go back," Nissa said. "The Tending won't wait, but the river will."

Wren folded the letter and put it inside her coat, against the canister's empty place.

"No," she said. "We go back and we sing."
## 7. The Hush

The Hush began where the glassgrass ended.

There was no border, no wall. The silver trees simply stopped, and the ground became grey, and the sound went out of the world. Wren stood at the edge with a rope around her waist, and Nissa stood behind her with the other end belayed around a stone pine.

"Twenty minutes," Nissa said. "Then I pull you back whether you like it or not."

"Twenty-five."

"Twenty." Nissa's mouth was a flat line. "I'm not losing two Alks in one life."

Wren walked in.

The first thing she lost was the wind. Then the sound of her own footsteps. Then, gradually, the memory of what her footsteps had sounded like. The grey was not fog; it had no texture, no temperature, no smell. It was like walking through the inside of an unanswered question. Shapes loomed and resolved into buildings: a house with no roof, a wall with a window, a cart with no wheels. They were familiar in the way a word is familiar before you remember what it means.

She kept her hand on the rope. She counted her steps. She did not answer the voices.

They came anyway.

*Wren,* said a woman's voice, somewhere to her left. *Wren, the water's cold.*

*Count the steps,* said a man's voice, behind her. *Count the steps, girl.*

*Don't answer,* said her own voice, from somewhere ahead.

She walked. The grey thinned, then thickened, then thinned again. She came to a place where the ground was covered in small grey stones, and each stone was humming, very faintly, a different note. She stopped.

"Tam," she said.

The grey in front of her moved. A boy stood there.

He was fourteen. He was made of grey light, and his edges were soft, and his face was almost right. He looked at her with an expression she remembered from a hundred evenings: half pleased, half uncertain, waiting to be told what to do.

"Wren," he said. "You're tall."

She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong in the Hush, flat and soundless.

"I got old."

"You're not old. You're tall." He looked around at the grey. "I've been here a long time. I kept calling you. Did you hear me?"

"I heard you."

"You didn't answer."

"No." Her throat closed. "I couldn't."

"That's all right." He said it simply, the way he had said everything at fourteen. "I think I was supposed to stop calling. I just didn't know how."

The Choirmaster came out of the grey behind him.

It was smaller here, or the Hush made it seem so. The burned robe was gone. It stood with its pronged hands folded, its bell-plate face tilted, and it did not move closer.

"He is not ready," it said. "None of them are ready. I have tried to teach them to be still."

"You kept them," Wren said.

"I kept them." The voices were quieter here, almost one voice. "I was afraid of the silence. I am still afraid. If I let them go, what am I?"

"A conductor," Wren said. "At the end of the piece."

The Choirmaster was silent.

Tam looked at Wren. "Are you going to sing?"

"Yes."

"Can I hear it?"

She knelt in the grey stones and took his grey hands. They were not cold. They were not anything.

"I can't take you back," she said. "I don't know how. I don't think anyone knows how. But I can give you something to keep."

"What?"

"The river," she said. "The way it looked in the evening, when the light came under the bridge and the fish rose. The ferry rope against the pole. Father singing badly in the boat. Mother laughing at him. You and me on the bank with our feet in the water, and the whole valley ringing behind us like a bell."

She gave it to him. She did not know how; she held his hands and remembered it as hard as she could, every detail, the cold, the smell, the sound, and she felt something go out of her and into him, like a note passing from one throat to another.

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was smiling.

"That's good," he said. "That's a good one."

The grey stones began to hum. The chord came up through Wren's knees, through her hands, through the rope at her waist. She sang the three notes: the first, the second, the third flattened, the chord that wanted to resolve.

The Hush breathed out.

It was not a dramatic thing. There was no light, no wind. The grey simply became less grey, and the stones rang, and somewhere far away a door opened, and the sound of water came through it, and then the sound of a bell, cracked and thin and still ringing.

Tam was gone. The Choirmaster was gone. Wren was alone in the grey with the rope around her waist, and then the rope pulled, hard, and she was running, and Nissa was shouting her name from the edge of the trees, and the world came back in a rush of silver and wind and the smell of cold pine.

She fell on the glassgrass and lay there, breathing.

"Did you find her?" Nissa asked.

"No," Wren said. "I found Tam."
## 8. The Chord

They reached Bellhollow at dusk, and the town was already in the basin.

The Tending had begun. Lanterns in every window, a fire in the center of the basin, the Long Bell hanging above it all with its lip worked in reeds and waves. Sable stood on the steps in her slate coat, and beside her was a small table with a jar on it, and beside the jar was an iron mallet.

The crowd turned as Wren came down the terrace steps. Nissa was on one side, Brannoc on the other; the Reedfolk had come up from the water and stood at the back with their lamps.

"Stop," Wren said.

Sable looked at her. Her face was tired, and old, and for a moment Wren saw what she would look like dead.

"You're too late," Sable said. "The Tending has begun."

"Then end it differently." Wren walked into the basin. "I went into the Hush. I found my brother. I found the Choirmaster. I found my mother's letter." She held it up. "The Note is a debt. Every voice in that bell is a hole in the world, and the Hush is the shape of the holes. You have been feeding it for four hundred years and calling it safety."

The crowd stirred. Sable did not move.

"The law says the Warden chooses," Wren said. "You chose. You chose a boy who was already gone. You chose eleven names. You chose my mother's silence. And you are going to choose again tonight, and I am asking you to choose the harder thing."

"Which is?"

"To open the bell."

The basin was silent.

"Open it," Sable said, "and the Note fails. The Hush comes. We lose the valley."

"Maybe." Wren's voice was steady. "Or maybe the valley learns to sing. My mother gave back eleven voices. The Hush is thinner than it was. It is not a wall, Sable. It is a debt, and debts can be paid. Not with one voice. With many."

"With what voices?"

"Ours." Wren turned to the crowd. "Not taken. Not chosen. Given. The chord is three notes. Anyone can sing them. It does not have to be perfect. It has to be willing."

Nissa stepped forward. She took the knotted cord from around her neck and held it up so everyone could see the three knots.

"I'll start," she said, and sang.

Her voice was not beautiful. It was a reed-reader's voice, rough and plain, but the three notes came out true: the first, the second, the third flattened. The chord hung in the air.

Brannoc sang next. His voice was a low rumble, half an octave down, and it should not have fit, and it did. Then the Reedfolk, in their long boats, a dozen voices. Then a woman on the second terrace. Then the baker. Then the children.

Sable stood on the steps with the mallet in her hand and did not sing.

Wren climbed the steps to her. The bell hung above them, huge and green-black, and the note inside it was so thin now that Wren could hear the individual voices, thousands of them, waiting.

"You can ring the holding stroke," Wren said. "The Note will hold. The valley will live. And in ten years, or twenty, someone else's brother will be chosen, and you will be older, and it will be easier, and the debt will be a little deeper. Or you can ring the giving stroke, and the bell will crack, and the voices will go home, and we will have to learn to be a valley that sings."

Sable looked at the bell. She looked at the crowd. She looked at Wren.

"Your mother said you would break the arithmetic," she said.

"I'm trying."

Sable's mouth trembled. Then she lifted the mallet.

She struck the giving stroke.

The bell did not ring. It *opened*. The note came out of it all at once, not a sound but a pressure, a wave that knocked Wren to her knees and blew out every lantern in the basin and cracked the tower from lip to shoulder with a noise like a mountain clearing its throat. The Long Bell split down the middle. The pieces hung there, ringing, and out of them came the voices.

They came like birds. They came like water. They came in a crowd, thousands of them, old and young, singing and shouting and weeping, and they rose over the basin and spread out across the valley, and where they passed the grey went out of the air.

Wren lay on the steps with her hands over her ears and her eyes open. She saw her brother's voice go up, a boy-shaped brightness, laughing. She saw a woman-shaped brightness that might have been her mother turn once in the air and look at her and then go on. She saw the Choirmaster at the edge of the crowd, tall and grey and very still, and then it was not there.

The last of the voices went out over the western road, and the valley was quiet.

Sable was on her knees beside the bell, the mallet still in her hand. Blood ran from her ear where the copper plate had split. She was looking at Wren, and her mouth was moving, and Wren could not hear her.

"What?" Wren said. Her own voice was far away, as if she were hearing it through water.

Sable tried again. Then she laughed, or cried, and put her hand over her mouth, and Wren understood.

The Warden of the Note could not hear the Note anymore.

Wren crawled to her and took her hand. Around them, the town was getting up. The fire had gone out. The tower stood, cracked, holding its broken bell. Somewhere on the second terrace, a child began to cry, and then a woman began to laugh, and then the whole basin was full of people making ordinary sounds, small and human, testing them like coins.

The spring under the town, which had been failing for sixty years, came back at dawn. It came up through the old channels with a noise like a crowd arriving, and the water ran clear, and the reeds at the north channel turned green.

---

The repairs took a season.

The tower was shored with quarry stone and new timber. The broken bell was left where it hung, split from lip to shoulder. It was honest. It had opened.

Sable lived in the Warden's house with the copper plate off her ear, learning to read lips and to write in a slate book. She was not forgiven, exactly. The town did not know how to forgive her, and she did not ask. But people brought her bread, and the children learned to face her when they spoke, and Brannoc came every week to shout at her about the quarry, which she seemed to enjoy.

Wren stayed. The valley needed a Listener, and the Listening House needed someone to go into the Undersong and give back the voices one at a time.

They did it together, in the end: Wren and Nissa and Brannoc and a rotating handful of townspeople who wanted to learn the chord. They read the names on the copper lids, sang the three notes, and let the voices go. Some went easily. Some fought. Some asked to stay. The shelves were still full, and would be for years.

The Tenders kept the lanterns and the rites, because a town needs ceremonies, but the Tending became a season of singing, four times a year, when anyone who wanted stood in the basin and made the chord. It was always imperfect. Someone was always flat. The children made up words. It was, Wren thought, the first honest sound the valley had made in four hundred years.

She wrote a letter, and left it in the Listening House, on the shelf with her mother's notebooks.

*To the one who comes after,*

*If you are reading this, you can hear the undertone. You are not mad. You are not cursed. You are a Listener, and the valley has been waiting for you.*

*The Note is gone. The bell is cracked. The Hush is thinner, but it is not gone; it is a debt, and we are still paying it. The voices are still coming home. Some of them are angry, and some of them are lost, and some of them have learned to sing again. The Undersong is open. The door under the north terrace is not locked.*

*There are three things I could not do.*

*I could not find my mother. She went into the Hush to give the voices back, and she is still there, or she is not, and I have stopped being able to tell the difference. If you find her, tell her I put her in the reeds, not the bell. Tell her the valley sang.*

*I could not answer the question the Cantors asked, the one that broke them. What do we owe the dead? I think the answer is: not everything. Not our whole lives. Not the next child. Enough to remember them, and not so much that we stop living. I think that is the chord. I am not sure. I am still learning.*

*The quarry is open again. The Guild found something under the deep shaft, a door with a bell on it, and Sable says it should be left alone, and Brannoc says it should be opened, and they are both right, which is the worst kind of argument.*

*Come down the road. The gate is open. The ferry runs. The kettle-birds are arguing.*

*The valley will wait.*

*— Wren Alk, Listener of Carrow Vale*

---

Twelve years later, on a clear autumn evening, Wren stood on the ninth switchback of the road into Carrow Vale and looked down at the town.

The tower was still cracked. The copper roofs were still green. The reeds at the north channel had spread farther than anyone expected, and the ferry was tied up at the dock with a lantern on its pole, and someone was singing badly in the square.

She was twenty-nine years old. She had a reed-cutter's hook at her belt and an empty copper canister in her pack. She had a letter in her pocket for a person she had not met.

Below her, on the twelfth switchback, a traveler had stopped to look at the valley.

Wren watched them for a moment. Then she started down the road to meet them.
