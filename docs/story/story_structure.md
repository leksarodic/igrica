# Story Structure

*How the novella works, how the game is built on it, and what the player is allowed to discover when.*

---

## Part one: the novella

**Length:** 8,996 words, eight chapters.
**POV:** third person limited, Wren Alk, past tense.
**Time span:** six days in late autumn, year 600.
**Standalone promise:** a reader who never plays the game gets a complete story with a resolution: the bell is opened, the voices go home, Sable is deafened, Wren stays. The central mystery is answered. The ending is hopeful without being tidy.

### Chapter map

| # | Chapter | Function | Reveal | Emotional beat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Road Down | Arrival; establish the valley, the note, the Hush, the Tending, Sable | The Note is failing | A stranger comes home and does not belong |
| 2 | Reedwater | Meet Nissa; establish the Hush as a debt; find the chord | The chord exists; the Hush can imitate | Grief shared is bearable |
| 3 | The Bell-Founder | Brannoc; humstone; the jar | Tam's voice was fed to the bell; Mira built a chord | The town's kindness is complicity |
| 4 | What Sable Kept | Pell's history; Sable's confession; the Undersong key | The Harvests; Tam's fate; Mira's plan; the Choirmaster | Rage without a clean target |
| 5 | The Undersong | The Vault; the Choirmaster; the jar | The Note is a prison of voices; the Choirmaster is lonely, not evil | Horror and pity at once |
| 6 | Glasswood | Mira's camp and letter | The Hush can be paid; Mira chose to stay; the chord is a practice, not a spell | Grief becomes a decision |
| 7 | The Hush | Tam; the memory gift; the chord in the grey | The dead need release, not rescue | Letting go as an act of love |
| 8 | The Chord | The Tending; the town sings; Sable opens the bell | The arithmetic can be broken; the cost is real | Joy and loss in the same breath |

The chapter order is a descent and return: valley rim → water → fire → archive → underground → stone forest → grey → home. It mirrors the hero's journey without ever using the language of destiny.

### Themes

- **Inheritance and complicity.** The Tenders did not invent the Harvests; they inherited them and kept them running. The novella is interested in the ordinary people who maintain an atrocity by calling it maintenance.
- **Consent and the arithmetic.** Sable's argument is real: one voice for a valley. The story does not refute the arithmetic with a slogan; it shows that the arithmetic was always incomplete, because it counted the wrong things.
- **Memory versus preservation.** The bell preserves voices by taking them out of the world. The Hush is what that preservation leaves behind. The chord returns voices by letting them go. The story asks what a memorial is for.
- **Grief and attention.** Wren's guilt is not that she failed to save Tam; it is that she stopped listening. The cure is not forgiveness but attention.
- **Institutions and intimacy.** The valley is saved by a town singing badly together, not by a hero. The most important line in the book is not a spell; it is an invitation to sing.

### Symbols

- **The bell** — safety bought with someone else's voice; also a container for grief.
- **The reed** — return, permeability, the dead given back to the water.
- **The chord** — plurality, imperfection, consent; three notes that want to resolve.
- **The jar** — preservation as capture; a voice with the person removed.
- **The hand** — Wren lets go of Tam's hand in the fog; she takes Sable's hand at the end.
- **Counting** — Wren's control mechanism; the thing she does instead of feeling. At the end she stops counting and starts singing.
- **The crack** — not a flaw to be repaired but an opening. The tower and the bell are both cracked; the valley survives because of it.

### Voice rules

- Wren narrates in concrete, physical detail. She does not name her emotions; she notices what her hands are doing.
- Sable speaks in complete, formal sentences. She avoids contractions when she is defending herself.
- Nissa speaks in interruptions, jokes, and river metaphors. She is the only character who teases Wren out of a spiral.
- Brannoc speaks in workshop metaphors and loud affection.
- Pell speaks in citations and asides.
- The Choirmaster speaks in borrowed phrases, half a beat out of sync.
- Mira, in letters, is warm, exact, and unsentimental. She never asks for pity.
- The prose avoids: prophecy, chosen-one language, "ancient evil," generic medieval fantasy, gore, and villain monologues. The horror is administrative and the hope is practical.

## Part two: the game

**Structure:** seven chapters, roughly 12–18 hours for a first-time explorer, 8–10 for a focused run. The novella's events are backstory, not a playable prologue. The player arrives twelve years later and deals with the aftermath.

### Chapter and quest map

1. **The Cracked Bell** (Bellhollow)
   - Tutorial: movement, Listen, combat, crafting, building, save.
   - Main quest: *A Door That Rings* — investigate why the Quarry Door is ringing.
   - Companion: Nissa joins at the ferry.
   - Choice: none yet; the town is established.
   - Reveal: the Returning are appearing on the west road; the Choir is not gone.

2. **The Sunken Choir** (Reedwater)
   - Main quest: *The Sunken Choir* — recover the Choir Chime to clear the north channel.
   - Dungeon: Sluice, Echo Maze, Hollow Stag.
   - Choice: give the Chime to the Tenders, the Reedfolk, or keep it.
   - Side quests: *Pip's Bell*, *The Ferryman's Debt*.
   - Reveal: the Chime is a Cantor tuning fork; the Choir's jars are still active.

3. **The Quarry Door** (Kestrel Crags)
   - Main quest: *The Deep Shaft* — the Guild has breached a sealed reliquary.
   - Dungeon: the Quarry Door, the Keeper.
   - Choice: open, study, or seal the door.
   - Reveal: the Cantors left a functioning tuning floor and a First Bell that has never rung.
   - Companion: Brannoc joins as a temporary defender.

4. **Mira's Letter** (Glasswood)
   - Main quest: *What the Reeds Remember* — follow Mira's trail.
   - Puzzles: Listening Trees, the Glass Observatory.
   - Reveal: Mira is (probably) alive in the Hush; the chord can return voices; the Choir is reforming.
   - Choice: tell Wren everything, or protect her from the worst of it.

5. **The Vault of Voices** (Undersong)
   - Main quest: *The Voice Lattice* — solve the Vault's logic and decide what to do with the jars.
   - Dungeon: the Cathedral of Columns, the Vault, the Choir.
   - Reveal: the Cantors' final order; the player's own voice can complete the Choir; the First Bell can be rung.
   - Choice: free the jars one by one, take the Choir's offer, or seal the Vault.

6. **What the Silence Kept** (The Hush)
   - Main quest: *The Far Grey* — find Mira, or find what is left of her.
   - Gameplay: navigation by memory, the Returning, the Choirmaster's hall.
   - Reveal: the Hush is a debt, not an enemy; the final choice is framed as a question, not a boss.
   - Choice: the three endings are unlocked here.

7. **The Long Note** (Bellhollow / the valley)
   - Finale: the Tending/Chord; the ending; the epilogue.
   - The player's choices determine who stands where, what the town looks like, and which verses of the epilogue appear.

### The three endings

**The Chord.** Rebuild the Chimeworks as a communal instrument. Return the jars by name. Free Mira, or let her choose. The valley becomes plural, noisy, and less stable; the Hush recedes but does not vanish. The town learns to sing four times a year. Cost: the old certainty is gone; some people leave; the Choir's voices are scattered and some are angry.

**The Note.** Restore the Note with a willing voice. The player can refuse to let anyone volunteer; if they accept, a character offers (never a child; the game refuses that option explicitly). The valley is stable for another generation. The cost is a person, a secret, and a debt deferred. Sable's argument wins, and she hates it.

**The Silence.** Let the Hush pass. Evacuate the low terraces, move Bellhollow upslope, and become a culture that travels with the seasons. The valley becomes wild; the Chimeworks are abandoned; the Returning are welcomed. Cost: the town as it was is gone; the Tenders' archive is lost; the player's home is a boat.

All three are bittersweet. None is "good" or "bad." The epilogue varies by faction trust, companions, side quests, and whether the player told the truth about Mira.

### Mystery schedule

| Layer | Question | Answered in |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | Why is the valley going quiet? | Game ch. 1–2 |
| Town | What is the Tending really for? | Game ch. 2–3 (the novella answers it for the reader) |
| Family | What happened to Tam? What did Sable do? | Novella; game ch. 4 |
| World | What is the Note made of? What is the Hush? | Novella; game ch. 4–5 |
| Deep | Is Mira alive? Can the debt be paid? | Game ch. 5–6 |
| Final | What do we owe the dead? | Game ch. 7, by the player's choice |

The game assumes the player has **not** read the novella. It re-reveals the town secret through play, but it never repeats the novella's scenes. The novella is a standalone artifact found in the world (the Listening House sells a bound copy), and reading it changes dialogue, not access.

### Adaptation principles

- **Show consequences, not cutscenes.** The cracked bell, Sable's deafness, the new window in the Listening House, the seasonal Chord, the Returning: the novella's ending is the game's environment.
- **Do not resurrect the novella's climax.** The player does not re-ring the bell or re-fight the Choirmaster. They deal with a valley that is still paying the debt.
- **Keep the moral question open.** The game does not tell the player which ending is correct. Characters argue; the world changes; the epilogue is written in the player's choices.
- **Let children follow the adventure.** The surface plot is clear: a valley is going quiet, a town is arguing, a friend is missing, and you can help. The deeper themes are there for adults without being required.
- **No lore dumps.** Every revelation arrives through a place, an object, a letter, or an argument. The journal records facts; it does not explain the theme.
