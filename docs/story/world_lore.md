# World Lore — Carrow Vale

*Canon reference. The novella is the primary source; where this document and the novella differ, the novella wins and this document is corrected.*

---

## 1. The valley

Carrow Vale is a glacial bowl in a wet, temperate upland, open to the west onto a drowned lowland called the Low Water. It is roughly twelve kilometres across. Three terraces of limestone and shale step down from the eastern heights to the river, and the river braids through a wide bed of reed and shallow channel before sliding out through a gorge to the sea.

The valley is shaped, in the literal sense, like a bell. The Cantors noticed this before they noticed anything else about it.

The climate is late autumn for most of the game: low amber sun, long shadows, mist in the low ground, rain that comes in from the west. Winters are wet and mild at the valley floor and sharp on the crags. The valley grows rye, reed, root vegetables, orchard apples, and a great deal of nothing that cannot be eaten. Its wealth has always been water, stone, and copper.

**Bellhollow** sits in the dry basin of an ancient waterfall at the valley's centre. The town is built on three terraces around the basin, with the Hollow Bell — a stone tower shaped like a bell — at the heart. Above the town, the eastern cliffs rise to the quarry road and the Kestrel Crags. Below and west, the land flattens into Reedwater. South, a broken stair climbs to the Glasswood. Beneath everything, the Undersong.

The valley's roads are old. Some are Cantor roads, cut to widths that no longer make sense, with drainage channels that still work after four hundred years of neglect. Others are farm tracks, boardwalks, and ferry routes. The west road is the only way in or out by land, and it runs along the edge of the Hush.

## 2. The Cantors

The Cantors arrived in the valley about six hundred years before the game. They were not a nation and not a religion. They were a guild culture of surveyors, founders, acousticians, and engineers, organized into houses named for intervals: the House of the Fifth, the House of the Third, the House of the Octave, and so on. They measured everything. They believed, or claimed to believe, that a place could be understood completely if it could be measured completely.

They discovered the Undersong, the great resonant cavity beneath the valley, and they built the **Chimeworks**: a landscape-scale instrument of cut channels, tuned stone chimes, resonant shafts, and one great bronze bell. The Chimeworks did not create the valley's note. It held it steady, the way a frame holds a string.

For a hundred and seventy years, the Chimeworks worked. The valley's springs ran clear, the ground was stable, the Hush — which the Cantors called *the Unmeasured* — did not exist in any record before the First Dissonance.

### The First Dissonance

In the year 180, a crack opened in the Chimeworks. The records blame an earthquake; later records blame a maintenance failure; a few Cantor letters blame a quarrel between the House of the Fifth and the House of the Octave over the tuning of the north channel. All three may be true. The crack let the valley's note slip. The Hush came through it.

The Cantors tried to retune. They failed. Then **Ilse Vey**, conductor of the House of the Octave, discovered that a human voice given to the great bell would strengthen the Note. Not a song. A voice, taken at the moment it was most itself — grief, terror, dying — and sealed in humstone.

The first gifts were volunteers. The practice was called **the Gift**, and it was spoken of as an honour. It began in 185, became a levy by 190, and a hunt by 195. The Cantors called the period the **Harvests**. The valley called it nothing, because the valley was not consulted.

### The Long Silence

In 200, the Cantors stopped speaking. The records do not explain it. The wax tablets from the last year are mostly lists: names, dates, weights of copper, the tuning of the north channel. Then a single line, in three different hands: *We will not be the bell.*

They went into the Undersong and sealed the door behind them. Some of the town's founders believed they were ashamed. Others believed they had gone mad. A few believed they were still down there, conducting. The game does not settle this completely; the Choirmaster is evidence, not proof.

The survivors above founded Bellhollow. They kept the bell, the rituals, and the spring. They forgot the rest on purpose. The annual rite of maintenance became **the Tending**, and the Tending became a story about gratitude.

## 3. The Long Note

The Long Note is not a song. It is a single sustained chord, held in the great bell and the Undersong, that keeps the valley's geology and hydrology stable. The bell is fed by the spring; the spring is shaped by the bell; the whole system is a loop. When the loop weakens, the Hush grows.

The Note has been fed with voices on and off for four hundred years. Most years, no one is asked. The Tenders keep a shelf of **listening jars** — sealed humstone vessels tuned to a single voice — and a Warden decides when a jar is needed. The town knows the Tending is solemn. It does not know the jars are people.

The Note fails for two reasons: the spring is silting and the bell is old. By the novella, it is "thin." After the novella, the bell is cracked and silent, and the valley holds its note communally, imperfectly, by singing the **Chord** four times a year.

### The Chord

Three notes: the first, the second, and a flattened third. It is not a beautiful chord. It is a chord that wants to resolve. The Cantors used it as a maintenance signal; Mira Alk rediscovered it as an act of return. Singing the chord while naming a taken person releases their voice from the bell or the Vault and returns it to the Hush, thinning the debt.

The chord is not magic. It is a contract, made audible. The valley's system responds to willing voices because the Cantors built it to respond to voices; the difference between a taken voice and a given one is the whole moral argument of the game.

## 4. The Hush

The Hush is not a place, a god, or a monster. It is what remains when a voice is taken and not returned: an accumulation of absence. It gathers in low ground, in ruins, in places where people have stopped listening. It drains sound, colour, warmth, and memory. It is not malicious. It wants, the way a debt wants.

The Hush can be paid. A voice at a time, by name, with the chord. It can also be fed, which makes it stronger and the Note stronger at the same time, because both are made of the same debt. This is the trap the Cantors fell into and the Tenders inherited.

**The rule:** never answer a voice you cannot see. The Hush can imitate. It builds a copy from the shape of a call, not the person. Answering gives it a thread to pull. The rule is not superstition; it is a survival protocol.

By the game, the Hush is thinner than it was but more active. The released voices did not all rest. Some are angry, some are lost, some have learned to sing again. A new phenomenon, **the Returning**, appears at the edges: patches of colour and sound that move against the wind, sometimes shaped like people, sometimes like nothing at all. The Reedfolk treat them as neighbours. The Tenders treat them as a security problem. Both are partly right.

The **Choirmaster** was Ilse Vey, transformed by four hundred years of holding the Vault. It is gone after the novella, dispersed when the bell opened, but its role remains: something in the Vault is still arranging voices. In the game, the Choir is reforming under a new conductor, and the player must decide what a choir is for.

## 5. Humstone and the Chimeworks

**Humstone** is a dark, fine-grained stone found in the deep quarry. It resonates with a voice or a note and holds the memory of it. The Cantors used it for bells, jars, chime columns, and the tuning forks carried by their surveyors.

Humstone is not infinite. The deep quarry is the only known source. Mining it is dangerous, both physically and because the stone remembers. A badly cut block can "speak" for years, replaying fragments of the quarry floor. The Quarry Guild knows this and works around it. The Tenders believe the deep quarry should be sealed. This disagreement is one of the game's central political tensions.

The Chimeworks are half-ruined. Some channels still carry water. Some chimes still ring when the wind is right. The Undersong gallery is the best-preserved section. The **Quarry Door**, breached in the game's opening, is a sealed Cantor reliquary beneath the deepest shaft; behind it is the original tuning floor and a bell that has never been rung.

## 6. Ecology

The valley's wildlife is musical and mostly harmless:

- **Kettle-birds** — round marsh birds that hum in fifths; kept as pets, terrible thieves, beloved.
- **Hush-hares** — grey hares that make no sound at all; their silence is natural, not Hush-made.
- **Glasswing moths** — translucent moths that gather around resonance; their wings ring faintly.
- **Reed-eels** — long, harmless, delicious; a staple.
- **Bellflowers** — flowers that close when spoken to; used by Reedfolk to mark safe water.
- **Lantern moss** — bioluminescent moss used in the Undersong and in Hush lanterns.
- **Chime trees** — the petrified pines of Glasswood, which ring when struck.

Dissonant creatures are ordinary animals or guardians whose note has been tangled by the Hush. They are not evil. When defeated, they disperse into motes and a resolving chord. The game never shows gore.

## 7. Cultures and daily life

**Bellhollow** is a mixed town of ferry folk, reed-cutters, bell-founders, quarry workers, bakers, and scribes. Houses are timber-framed with ochre plaster and copper roofs. Meals are rye bread, eel, reed shoots, apple, and a fermented reed beer called *shale*. People greet each other by asking what they heard that morning. Funerals involve speaking the dead person's favourite sound aloud; the bell is not rung for the dead, because the bell is for the living note.

**The Reedfolk** are boat families who move between Reedwater and the Low Water. They paint wave-glyphs on their hulls, bury their dead in reed beds, and treat the Hush as a tide to be lived with rather than a wall to be held. They are hospitable, clannish, and slow to trust anyone who owns a house.

**The Quarry Guild** is a work culture: blunt, proud, dusty, generous with food and stingy with sentiment. They wear dust veils and use hand signs in the cutting yards because the stone sings. They believe the valley should pay for its own upkeep, and they resent being told to wait by people who have never swung a hammer.

**The Tenders** are the ritual and archival class. They keep the bell, the jars, the calendar, and the careful language. They are not villains; they are the people who inherited a terrible job and did it without asking whether it should exist.

**The Listeners** are not an organization. They are people born with an ear for the undertone, scattered through the valley and beyond. They share a tradition of notebooks, a handshake, and the rule about voices. Most never meet another Listener. The player is one.

## 8. Language and names

Carrow Vale names are short, concrete, and often borrowed from sound, water, or work: Wren, Sable, Nissa, Brannoc, Pell, Mira, Tam, Corin, Maud, Orrin, Perrin, Vess, Kettle, Quill, Alk, Voss. Cantor names are longer and more formal: Ilse Vey, Ovin, Nema, Sera. Cantor inscriptions use interval marks rather than letters; the Tenders' script is a simplified descendant.

Common phrases:

- *"What did you hear?"* — a morning greeting.
- *"The valley will wait."* — said at parting, and at funerals, and by the game when you stop.
- *"Don't answer it."* — the first rule.
- *"A chord, not a note."* — Mira's heresy, now the town's practice.
- *"Reed, not bell."* — a Reedfolk funeral blessing; also Wren's promise to her father and mother.

## 9. The game's present (year 612)

Twelve years after the novella. Wren Alk is twenty-nine and lives in Bellhollow as the valley's Listener. Sable Voss is seventy, deaf, and no longer Warden in anything but name; a Tender council governs with her counsel. Nissa Quill runs the ferry and speaks for the Reedfolk. Brannoc Kettle is seventy-seven and mostly retired; his daughter Maud runs the Quarry Guild. Pell is ninety-two and still cataloguing.

The spring runs clear. The Hush is thinner but restless. The Tenders sing the Chord four times a year; the town is proud of it and bad at it. The tower is shored, the Long Bell still split and silent. The Listening House has a new window.

The Quarry Guild has opened the deep shaft and found a sealed door with a bell on it. Sable says leave it. Maud says the valley needs the humstone. Brannoc says they are both right, which is the worst kind of argument. Then the door begins to ring on its own, and the Returning appear in greater numbers, and a new Listener comes down the road.

That is where the game begins.
