# Three Concepts, One Game

*Phase 1 creative direction — internal studio document*
*The Long Note Studio, November 2026*

This document records three genuinely different game concepts developed before any code was written. Each was judged against the same questions: does it have a world worth remembering, a mystery worth solving, a loop that supports both a twenty-minute session and a long evening, and a production shape a small team can actually finish on Mac and iPhone?

---

## Concept A — *Saltglass*

**Pitch.** A caravan of glassmakers crosses a desert of fused sand around the impact site of a fallen moon. The glass remembers light: every shard holds an image of the sky at the moment it cooled, and some shards hold images of things that have not happened yet.

**World and visual identity.** A white-gold desert under a sky with two suns, broken by black obsidian mesas and the ribbed skeleton of the fallen moon. Everything is translucent, faceted, and warm. Caravans are built from salvaged mirror panels. Architecture is wind-cut: no right angles, only flow. The palette is bleached bone, honey, and deep cobalt shadow, with hard refracted rainbows used sparingly as a navigation language.

**Central mystery.** The moon did not fall. It was *sent* — and the shards are pieces of a message that is still arriving. The glassmakers have been reading the message for generations without realizing that each reading changes it.

**Reason to explore.** Every glass formation is a lens onto a different time. The desert is a library that must be walked through. Caravans leave cairns of mirror-glass that show you where water was, or will be.

**Core loop.** Travel between oases → read glass for routes, weather, and history → trade images and lenses → solve light-and-reflection puzzles to open buried sites → survive duststorms and glass predators → return to the caravan with a new fragment of the message.

**How the systems fit.** Combat is ranged and positional: you fight by redirecting light with lenses, and the environment is your weapon, so combat and puzzle-solving share a vocabulary. Crafting is lens-grinding and mirror assembly. Exploration is navigation by refraction. Puzzles are literal perspective puzzles: align shards, split beams, use heat shimmer as a lens. Building means raising mirror towers that re-angle sunlight across the desert, changing routes permanently.

**Emotional atmosphere.** Awe and heat. Loneliness with moments of startling beauty. The feeling of reading a letter written by someone you will never meet.

**Why it fits Mac and iPhone.** Touch is excellent for lens manipulation and dragging light. Sessions are naturally short: one oasis, one reading, one route. Visuals are cheap to render (flat translucent planes, emissive glass) and scale well.

**Why it was not chosen.** The light-and-lens mechanic is beautiful but narrow. It makes every problem a beam-alignment problem, and the desert offers little environmental variety over a full game. It is also the hardest concept to make emotionally warm; the world is gorgeous but distant. And a game about reading the future risks becoming a game about being told what to do next.

## Concept B — *The Cartographer's Wake*

**Pitch.** An archipelago that rearranges itself whenever it is accurately mapped. You are a surveyor for a failing maritime academy, sent to chart waters that refuse to stay charted, and to find the one island that stays put.

**World and visual identity.** A green-and-slate sea of steep islands, tidal caves, and drowned towns. Maps are physical objects in the world: enormous brass surveying engines, folded paper birds, ink that moves. The art direction is nautical folk-art: painted hulls, carved compass roses, weathered signal flags. Islands are hand-drawn silhouettes; the sea is a living textile of currents.

**Central mystery.** The archipelago is not moving. The *map* is. Someone is editing the world through its charts, and the academy has been doing it for two centuries without knowing. The fixed island at the center is the place where the first map was drawn — and where the eraser is kept.

**Reason to explore.** Surveying pays: accurate charts reveal resources, safe routes, and hidden anchorages. But the better you map an island, the sooner it disappears, so you must choose what to record and what to leave unknown. That tension is the heart of the game.

**Core loop.** Sail → survey an island with compass, sextant, and soundings → decide what to ink → solve cartographic and tidal puzzles → trade charts and rumors → confront the consequences of your own map when the island returns changed.

**How the systems fit.** Combat is ship-to-ship and boarding, with wind and current as tactical resources; on land, it is light skirmishing against wreckers and chart-pirates. Crafting is chart-making, instrument repair, and boat-building. Exploration is the game. Puzzles are triangulation, dead reckoning, tide timing, and map-reading — genuinely spatial and genuinely learnable. Building means establishing signal towers and anchorages that make routes safer for everyone, including rivals.

**Emotional atmosphere.** Salt wind, lantern light below deck, the vertigo of realizing the coastline is not where you left it. A melancholy of lost places and the stubborn human wish to write things down.

**Why it fits Mac and iPhone.** Sailing is calm and touch-friendly; map-drawing is a natural multitouch activity. Short sessions work well (survey one island). Low-poly islands with painted skies are cheap.

**Why it was not chosen.** It is the most technically ambitious concept: a procedural archipelago with persistent player-authored maps, naval AI, and sea simulation is a large risk for a small team. More importantly, its central loop is *administration* — surveying and inking — and the fantasy of being a mapmaker, while lovely, keeps the player at one remove from the world. The concept is strongest as a future game, not this one.

## Concept C — *The Long Note* (selected)

**Pitch.** A valley lives because a single note has been sounding for four hundred years. The note is failing. You are one of the few people who can hear what it is made of.

**World and visual identity.** Carrow Vale: a glacial bowl of terraced cliffs, reed water, petrified forest, and a town built inside the dry basin of an ancient waterfall. Bellhollow is ochre plaster and copper, lantern-lit, with a cracked stone bell tower at its center. The valley is tuned like an instrument: stone chimes on ridgelines, water channels cut to specific lengths, arches that shape wind into chords. Beyond the town, the Hush: a spreading grey country where sound dies, color drains, and memory thins. The palette is pewter dusk, reed gold, bone, verdigris, and ember, with the Hush rendered as a desaturated, almost monochrome field crossed by thin ribbons of returning color. Low-poly and flat-shaded, with hand-placed silhouettes: bell curves, tuning-fork trees, tall thin people in wide hats.

**Central mystery.** What is the Long Note, and what is the Hush? The town believes the Note protects them from silence. The truth is worse and sadder: the Note is a prison built from taken voices. The Hush is what those voices left behind. Every generation has been told that the valley is kept safe by a song; every generation has been trained not to ask who is singing it.

**Reason to explore.** The valley is a musical instrument built by a vanished culture, and it can be read. Inscriptions are chords. Architecture is a diagram. Water carries pitch. Every ruin is a sentence in a language the player learns by looking. Exploration is rewarded with understanding first and equipment second: a carved wall can teach you a puzzle solution three regions later.

**Core loop.** Arrive somewhere → listen and observe → gather materials and clues → solve a spatial, mechanical, or harmonic problem → open a path or restore a mechanism → fight or avoid what guards it → bring what you learned back to people who disagree about what it means. Combat, crafting, building, and puzzles all draw on the same verb: *tune*.

**How the systems fit.** Combat is readable action combat with tonal weaknesses: enemies are "dissonant" knots, and the player's weapons, chimes, and companion abilities each carry a pitch. Matching pitch is not a color-matching gimmick — it is expressed through attack timing, sound cues, and enemy behavior, and it is never required for basic competence. Crafting produces tuning forks, resonators, bridges, lanterns, and instrument upgrades from reeds, resin, copper, and humstone. Building is placement of a small vocabulary of resonant objects into sockets and open ground; a well-placed resonator can open a gate, calm a creature, or purify a patch of Hush. Puzzles are the spine: tuning stones, water sluices, echo mazes, chord sequences hidden in songs and carvings, and multi-stage archaeological mysteries. Every major dungeon has one mechanical idea the player has not seen before.

**Emotional atmosphere.** Autumn dusk, woodsmoke, the hush before a bell rings. Warmth against a large quiet. Grief handled gently. The eerie beauty of a place where sound has been taken. A hopeful ending that does not pretend loss can be undone.

**Why it fits Mac and iPhone.** The camera is a comfortable third-person orbit at a readable distance, which works with mouse-and-keyboard, controller, and touch. Puzzles are spatial but not twitchy. Sessions have natural stopping points at lanterns and region thresholds, with no timers. The art is low-poly, vertex-colored, and lit by one directional sun plus emissive accents, which is cheap on iPhone and beautiful on a Mac display. The interface is a field notebook that scales cleanly from a large monitor to a phone in landscape. Audio is central, but every audio cue has a visual twin, so the game is fully playable muted or by deaf players.

**Why it was chosen.** It has the strongest marriage of theme and mechanic: the world's central question (who pays for safety?) is also the thing you do with your hands (tune, listen, decide what to preserve). It supports handcrafted narrative and meaningful procedural side-caves without either swallowing the other. It is warm enough for a parent and child to play together and textured enough for adults to argue about. It is buildable by a small team: a stylized world of simple, confident shapes; a compact combat vocabulary; and puzzles made of logic rather than content volume.

**What we deliberately avoided.** A chosen-one prophecy. An ancient evil. Elves, orcs, or medieval Europe. A loot treadmill. A grey morality where every choice is equally bad. A villain who enjoys cruelty. The Hush is not evil; neither is the Warden who feeds it. The horror is ordinary: good people maintaining a system whose cost has been made invisible to them.

## Decision

**Selected: *The Long Note*.**

The remainder of this repository documents and builds that game. The two rejected concepts are retained here as evidence of the comparative process and as possible future projects; no work from them is reused.
